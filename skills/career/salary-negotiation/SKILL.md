# Salary Negotiation

Negotiation is the shortest, highest paid conversation in a hiring process, and
the one candidates prepare for least. This skill is about the sentences you say
under pressure. Read them aloud until they sound like you: a script you cannot
deliver calmly is worse than no script. Two caveats. Pay law varies by country,
state, province and city, and it changes, so treat every legal note here as a
prompt to check your jurisdiction rather than as legal advice. And no number
here is a market rate, because rates are local, dated and level specific.

## Let them name the number first

Whoever names a number first sets the frame everybody argues inside. A recruiter
asking your expectations early is doing budget qualification, not making an
offer, so a number you say then caps you before anyone has decided you are worth
hiring. Your leverage is lowest before they want you and highest after they have
chosen you and closed the other candidates. The goal is not evasion forever; it
is delay until an offer exists. "I'm flexible" is not politeness, it is a bid of
zero: it reads as "discount available" and gives the recruiter no reason to
move.

```text
Weak:  "I'm looking for around 120."
Weak:  "I'm currently on 95, so something above that."
Weak:  "I'm flexible, whatever's fair."

Strong: "I'd rather not anchor on a number before we both know the scope.
         What range has the team budgeted for this level?"
Strong: "You'll know the band better than I do. If you share it, I can tell
         you straight away whether we're in the same neighbourhood."
Strong: "Compensation matters to me, but fit matters more this early. Can we
         come back to it once we know we want to work together?"
```

## Deflect the push, twice, then answer on your terms

Some recruiters are required to log a number before advancing you. Deflect twice
with warmth, and if the third ask is real, answer with a researched range and a
reason, not a wish.

```text
Them: "I do need a figure to move your application forward."

Weak:   "OK, 115?"
Weak:   "Whatever you think is right."

Strong: "Understood. Based on what I'm seeing for this level in this market,
         roles like this land in the X to Y range, and I'd want to be in the
         upper part of that given [the specific scope you just described].
         That said, I'd look at the whole package, so if the base is fixed
         there may be other ways to get there."
```

Three properties make this safe. The range is sourced, so you can defend it. Its
top is your realistic target rather than a fantasy, because they will hear the
bottom. And the last sentence opens the package before base is locked. If they
ask what you currently earn, that is a worse question: your current pay reflects
your last negotiation, not this role's value. A growing number of jurisdictions
have restricted or banned asking for salary history, and the trend has been
toward more restriction, but rules differ by place and change over time, so
check what applies where you and the employer are.

```text
Them: "What's your current salary?"

Weak:   "I'm on 95 plus a small bonus."
Weak:   "I'd rather not say." (true, but a dead end)

Strong: "I'd rather leave my current package out of it. It reflects a
         different scope and a market from a few years ago. I'm looking at
         this role on its own terms, in the X to Y range."
Strong: "I think asking for salary history is restricted in some places now,
         and either way it isn't a great guide to this role. Happy to talk
         about my expectations for this job instead."
```

Never inflate a current salary figure. It is checkable in some markets, it is a
lie you carry into the job, and it is unnecessary when you can simply decline.

## Build a range you can defend out loud

A number you cannot source will collapse the moment it is questioned. Build the
range before the first call, and write down where each end came from. Sources
that hold up: posted ranges for the same title, level and location, including
ones employers publish under pay transparency rules where those apply;
crowdsourced compensation sites, useful for shape and spread but unreliable on
level mapping, so read the level definitions before trusting a row; recruiters
working your market, who see closed offers rather than asking prices, asked
"what are you seeing this close at" rather than "what's fair"; people doing the
job, asked "would a band of X to Y for a senior role here sound low, right, or
high?", which is answerable by someone who will not share their own pay; and
your own recent competing offers, the strongest data you will ever have.

Adjust for level, location and company stage before comparing. Hold three
numbers: your walk away, your target, and the number you ask for, which sits
above your target because the ask is the ceiling of the conversation. Never say
your walk away out loud.

## Negotiate the package, not the salary

Base is often the most constrained component: banded, comparable across the
team, and compounding. Treating base as the whole negotiation means hearing
"no" once and stopping. Separate the components:

- Base salary. Predictable, pensionable, the basis for future raises. Usually
  the hardest to move outside a band.
- Bonus. Target percentage, and crucially whether it is individual, company
  performance, or discretionary. Ask what it actually paid out for the last two
  cycles, and treat a discretionary bonus as a maybe, not as income.
- Equity. See below. The widest gap between what is said and what is received.
- Sign on. Often the easiest money in the building, because it comes from a
  different budget and does not disturb internal pay bands. Ask for it to cover
  something concrete: forfeited bonus, unvested equity, relocation, notice.
- Benefits and time. Pension matching, healthcare, leave, learning budget,
  equipment, parental policy. Some are fixed policy, some are not.

```text
Them: "The base is fixed at the top of the band for this level."

Weak:   "OK, I understand." (the conversation is now over)

Strong: "That's helpful, thank you, and I'll take the band as given. Since
         base is capped, could we look at a sign on bonus to bridge the gap?
         I'm leaving an unvested bonus behind, and a one time payment of Z
         would close it without touching the band."
```

## Understand the equity before you value it

This is where the most money is silently lost, because candidates accept a
headline "total comp" figure built on assumptions nobody stated. Ask these in
writing, and assign the equity no value until they are answered.

- How many units, and options or RSUs? Options give you the right to buy at a
  strike price; RSUs are shares delivered on vest.
- The vesting schedule and the cliff. A common shape is four years with a one
  year cliff, meaning nothing vests until month twelve, but schedules vary and
  some are back weighted into years three and four.
- For options: the strike price, the exercise window after you leave (often
  short, which can force an expensive purchase or total forfeiture), and any
  early exercise provision.
- For a private company, the total shares outstanding, so a grant of N units
  means something. A percentage and a share count are only comparable if you
  know the denominator.
- Preferred versus common. Investors typically hold preferred stock with a
  liquidation preference that pays out first. You hold common, so in a modest
  exit the preferred stack can absorb most of the proceeds.
- The valuation being quoted and its date. A price from the last funding round
  is a paper valuation of preferred shares, not a price anyone will pay you.
- Whether any liquidity exists: a public market, tender offers, secondaries, or
  none. Paper value you cannot sell is not money you can spend.
- What happens on acquisition, and whether vesting accelerates.

```text
Weak:   "And the equity is worth about 200k?"

Strong: "Could you put the grant details in writing: number of units, option
         or RSU, strike price if options, the vesting schedule and cliff,
         total shares outstanding, and the date and type of the valuation
         you're quoting? I want to understand it properly rather than guess."
Strong: "Has there been any liquidity event for common holders, a tender or
         secondary? I'm trying to work out how to weigh this against cash."
```

An offer that is mostly illiquid private equity is a bet. Value it at whatever
you would genuinely pay for it today, usually far below the headline, and
negotiate the cash accordingly.

## Get the offer in writing before you negotiate

A verbal offer is a conversation. Negotiating against remembered numbers means
arguing about what was said rather than what was offered, and details like
vesting, start date and bonus type quietly go missing. Do not react to the
number on the call: "thank you, this is exciting, I'd like to read it carefully
and come back to you" costs nothing and buys a day.

```text
Strong: "That's great to hear, thank you. Could you send the full details in
         writing: base, bonus target and how it's determined, the equity
         grant terms, sign on, start date and any relocation support? I'd
         like to read it properly before I respond."
```

## Make one counter, and make it good

Haggling in a loop burns goodwill and signals that no number will end the
conversation. Come back once with everything you want, justified. A counter has
five parts: enthusiasm, the specific asks, a reason per ask, flexibility about
how they get there, and a close that says yes if they do it.

```text
Weak:   "Is there any flexibility on the offer?"
Weak:   "I was hoping for more, can you do better?"
Weak:   "I need at least X or I'm out." (an ultimatum you may regret)

Strong: "Thank you for this, I'm genuinely keen and I'd like to make it work.
         There are two things I'd ask for. First, base at X: the scope here
         covers [specific responsibility], and what I'm seeing for that level
         in this market sits above the offer. Second, a sign on of Z to cover
         the bonus I forfeit by leaving in [month]. If base is constrained by
         the band, I'm open to weighting more of it into the sign on or the
         equity refresh instead. If we can get there, I'm ready to sign."
```

Two asks, three at most. Every extra item makes it look like a wish list and
invites a partial answer. Say the number, then stop talking. Silence after an
ask is uncomfortable and effective; filling it is how people negotiate against
themselves. Send it by email so your exact wording, not your nerves, is what
gets circulated internally for approval.

## Competing offers are leverage, and bluffing is not

A real competing offer is the strongest thing you can bring, because it is
verifiable pressure rather than opinion. Use it as a preference, not a threat.

```text
Weak:   "I have another offer for more, so you need to beat it."
Weak:   "I've got other options." (vague, and reads as a bluff)

Strong: "I should be straight with you: I have another offer in hand, and it's
         higher on base. You're my first choice on the work and the team, and
         if you can get to X I'll accept today and withdraw from the other
         process."
```

Never invent an offer. Recruiters talk, companies sometimes ask to see it, and
if they call the bluff with "we understand, good luck", you have no move left
that does not destroy your credibility. Timeline pressure from a real offer is
legitimate to share, so say the date rather than implying a threat.

## Negotiate the terms that are not money

These are frequently easier to win than base, and sometimes worth more. A later
start date protects unvested money, bonus dates and notice periods, and buys
rest, so ask plainly. Get any remote or hybrid arrangement into the written
offer as days and locations rather than "flexible", because policies change and
a written term survives a manager change better than an understanding does.
Title costs no cash and affects your next role's band, so be specific about
which title and why the scope matches it. And if they cannot move money now,
ask for a scheduled review with written criteria, because "ambitious but
achievable" without criteria is a promise you cannot enforce.

```text
Weak:   "Can we revisit salary in a few months?"

Strong: "If the band is fixed today, could we put a compensation review at six
         months into the offer letter, with the criteria we'd measure against
         written down? I'd rather agree what good looks like now than argue
         about it later."
```

## Exploding offers, and when to walk

An offer with a same day or 48 hour deadline is a pressure tactic far more often
than a genuine constraint. Answer it calmly and factually.

```text
Strong: "I want to give this the consideration it deserves and I can't do that
         by tomorrow. I can give you a firm answer by [date]. Does that work?"
Strong: "If the deadline is genuinely immovable, I understand, but I'd have to
         decline rather than accept something I haven't thought through."
```

Most deadlines move when tested. One that does not, on a role you would
otherwise want, is information about how the company will treat you later. Walk
when the number is below your walk away and they will not move; they refuse to
put agreed terms in writing; the process has been dishonest; the equity answers
are evasive; or the reaction to a polite, single, well reasoned counter is
hostility. That reaction previews your first disagreement as an employee. A
well made counter does not lose an offer. Offers are rescinded over aggressive
ultimatums, endless renegotiation, dishonesty, or negotiating after accepting.
Asking once, warmly and specifically, is normal and expected.

## Close it, in writing, then stop

When they meet the terms, confirm the exact numbers in writing and accept
clearly. Accepting in principle while still shopping is the fastest way to burn
a reputation in a small industry.

```text
Strong: "That works, thank you for making it happen. To confirm what I'm
         accepting: base X, target bonus P percent, sign on Z paid with the
         first cheque, grant of N units on the schedule described, start date
         the Nth, three days remote per week. Send the revised letter and
         I'll sign it today."
```

After acceptance: get the amended letter before resigning anywhere, do not
reopen the negotiation, withdraw politely from other processes rather than
ghosting, and keep the file. The offer letter, grant documents and research
notes are your baseline at the first review and your evidence next time.

Bad: accepting verbally on Friday, asking for another five thousand on Monday.
Good: one counter, one close, one signed letter that says exactly what you were
promised.
