Scripts and structure for negotiating a compensation package without putting
the offer at risk.

## What it does

Turns the compensation conversation into something you can rehearse. The skill
supplies the exact sentences for the moments that decide the number: the
recruiter's first "what are you looking for", the push when you deflect, the
counter, the close, and the deadline designed to rush you. Every script comes
in a weak and a strong version, because the difference between them is usually
worth more than anything else you do in the process.

It also covers the part candidates skip: reading an equity grant well enough to
know whether the headline number means anything, and negotiating the components
that move when base is capped.

Two things it deliberately does not do. It does not give legal advice, because
pay law varies by country, state, province and city and changes over time, so
every legal note is framed as something to check where you live. And it does
not print market rates, because a rate is only meaningful for one level in one
market at one moment; the skill teaches you to source your own instead.

## When to use this

- A recruiter has asked what you are looking for and you do not want to answer.
- An offer has landed and you want more of it without souring the relationship.
- You have equity in an offer and cannot tell what it is actually worth.
- You have a competing offer and want to use it without bluffing.
- You have been given a deadline shorter than your ability to think.
- You want something that is not money: a start date, remote days, a title, a
  review with written criteria.
- You have accepted and want to know what to do next, and what not to reopen.

Do not use this for interview performance itself. Getting the offer is a
different job, handled by the interview prep skill linked below. This skill
starts the moment the conversation turns to money.

## Quick start

One offer, one counter, one close.

The offer arrives by phone: base 100, 10 percent target bonus, no sign on, an
equity grant described as "significant", start in three weeks. You do not react
to the number. You say:

> "That's great to hear, thank you. Could you send the full details in writing:
> base, bonus target and how it's determined, the equity grant terms, sign on
> and start date? I'd like to read it properly before I respond."

It arrives in writing. Your research says the band tops out nearer 115 for this
scope, you forfeit a bonus by leaving in March, and the equity turns out to be
options in a private company with no liquidity, so you value it near zero and
negotiate the cash. You counter once, by email, with two asks:

> "Thank you for this, I'm genuinely keen and I'd like to make it work. There
> are two things I'd ask for. First, base at 115: the role covers the migration
> work and on call rotation we discussed, and what I'm seeing for that scope in
> this market sits above the offer. Second, a sign on of 8 to cover the bonus I
> forfeit by leaving in March. If base is constrained by the band, I'm open to
> weighting more of it into the sign on instead. If we can get there, I'm ready
> to sign."

They come back with 110 and the full 8 sign on. That clears your target. You
close it, in writing, with the terms spelled out:

> "That works, thank you for making it happen. To confirm what I'm accepting:
> base 110, target bonus 10 percent, sign on 8 paid with the first salary
> payment, the option grant on the four year schedule with a one year cliff as
> described, starting on the 6th. Send the revised letter and I'll sign it
> today."

Then you stop. You do not ask for another 2 on Monday.

## Key concepts

**Anchoring.** The first number spoken frames everything after it. A number you
give before an offer exists caps you before anyone has decided you are worth
hiring, which is why the goal early on is delay rather than evasion.

**The deflection ladder.** Deflect twice, then answer with a sourced range
rather than a point, with your realistic target at the top, because they will
hear the bottom. Add a sentence that opens the whole package before base gets
locked.

**Salary history is a different question.** What you currently earn reflects
your last negotiation, not this role's value. Many jurisdictions have
restricted or banned asking, and the trend has been toward more restriction,
but rules differ by place and change, so check what applies where you and the
employer are. The scripts let you decline without needing the law.

**The package.** Base, bonus, equity, sign on, benefits and time are separate
budgets with separate constraints. Sign on is often the easiest money in the
building because it does not disturb internal pay bands. Knowing this turns one
"no" into several open questions.

**Equity literacy.** Vesting, cliff, strike price, share count against total
outstanding, preferred versus common, and whether any liquidity exists at all.
A paper valuation from the last funding round prices preferred shares. You
hold common. In a modest exit the preferred stack can absorb most of the
proceeds, which is why the grant needs reading rather than admiring.

**One counter.** A single, warm, specific counter with a commitment attached
reads as professional. A haggling loop signals that no number will end the
conversation and burns the goodwill you will need on day one.

**Real leverage.** A competing offer you actually hold is verifiable pressure.
An invented one is a bluff a recruiter can call by saying "we understand, good
luck", after which you have no move left.

## Common pitfalls

**Answering the expectations question with a number.**
Bad: "I'm looking for around 120." Good: "What range has the team budgeted for
this level?" Why: the first number sets the ceiling of the negotiation, and
yours is guessed while theirs is budgeted.

**Saying you are flexible.**
Bad: "I'm flexible, whatever's fair." Good: "Compensation matters to me, but
fit matters more this early. Can we come back to it once we know we want to
work together?" Why: flexibility without a range is a bid of zero and gives the
recruiter no reason to move.

**Negotiating against a verbal offer.**
Bad: countering on the call from memory. Good: asking for the full terms in
writing first. Why: you end up arguing about what was said, and the details
nobody wrote down, like vesting and bonus type, quietly resolve against you.

**Treating the headline equity number as money.**
Bad: adding "200k of equity" to the offer's value. Good: asking for share
count, total outstanding, strike, preference and liquidity before valuing it at
anything. Why: a preferred share valuation is not a price anyone will pay you
for common stock you cannot sell.

**Stopping at the first no on base.**
Bad: "Okay, if the band is fixed, I understand." Good: "That makes sense. Given
base is capped, could we look at a sign on, an additional equity grant, or a
review at six months instead?" Why: base is the most constrained component, and
a no on base is not a no on the package.

**Haggling in rounds.**
Bad: three separate emails each asking for a bit more. Good: one counter with
every ask, each justified, and a close that commits. Why: repeated asks signal
that no number ends it, which makes people stop trying to find one.

**Bluffing a competing offer.**
Bad: "I've got other options" with nothing behind it. Good: naming a real offer
and committing to accept if they match, or saying nothing at all. Why: if they
decline, you must either walk or admit the bluff, and both are worse than not
having said it.

**Panicking at a deadline.**
Bad: signing in 24 hours to avoid losing it. Good: "I can give you a firm
answer by [date]. Does that work?" Why: most exploding deadlines move when
tested, and one that does not is telling you how the company handles
disagreement.

**Reopening after acceptance.**
Bad: accepting verbally, then asking for more the next day. Good: getting every
agreed term into the revised letter before you accept. Why: this is one of the
few behaviours that genuinely loses offers, and it costs you the relationship
as well as the money.
