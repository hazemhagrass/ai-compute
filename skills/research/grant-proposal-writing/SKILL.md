A grant proposal is not a paper. A reviewer of a paper has already been handed
the results; a reviewer of a proposal must decide, from promises alone, whether
to give you money to produce them. This skill covers what that difference
changes: how to write aims that survive a skim, how to argue significance to a
panel that knows your field but not your subfield, how to frame feasibility
without overclaiming, and how to read a rejection before resubmitting.

Four neighbouring skills own adjacent ground and are not restated here:

- [`truth-first`](../truth-first/SKILL.md) owns whether a single claim is
  grounded. Preliminary data and track-record claims are subject to its rules.
- [`technical-writing`](../../writing/technical-writing/SKILL.md) owns sentence
  craft: leading with the point, complete examples, writing for a reader in a
  hurry. A panel is exactly that reader.
- [`planning`](../../workflow/planning/SKILL.md) owns breaking the work into
  checkable stages, which is what a timeline and a measurement plan are.
- [`academic-paper-writing`](../academic-paper-writing/SKILL.md) owns the shape
  of the outputs a grant promises to produce.

## Read the evaluation criteria before writing anything

The funder publishes how proposals are scored. Most writers ignore it and write
the document they would want to read, then lose to people who answered the
scoring rubric directly. The criteria are the specification.

- Find the published review criteria and copy them into your outline, one
  section heading per criterion. If the criteria are Significance, Approach,
  Innovation, and Investigators, the proposal should make each one findable in
  under thirty seconds.
- Note the order the funder lists them in. Early items get more panel time;
  put your strongest case against the first-listed criterion.
- Read three funded abstracts from the same programme. They show the register
  the panel expects: how much preliminary data, how broad the frame, how
  specific the aims. Unfunded abstracts show the register that loses.
- Check the exclusion rules too: word limits, page limits, what counts against
  them, whether figures are allowed in the research plan, what CV format is
  required. Unforced formatting errors read as carelessness to a panel
  judging how carefully you will run the money.

**Bad:** writing the proposal, then checking whether it fits the call text.

**Good:** the call text's criteria become the proposal's section structure,
and every section opens by naming the criterion it serves.

## The one sentence project description

The whole project must survive compression to one sentence. If it cannot,
the aims are not one project; they are several projects sharing a grant
application, and the panel will notice they do not cohere.

Write the sentence first: "We will determine whether X causes Y, by Z, in
population W." Then test every section against it. An aim that does not serve
the sentence is either a second proposal or a pet idea; both are cuts, not
additions. A sentence with "and" joining two independent clauses is usually
two grants in one envelope.

**Bad:** "We will characterise the protein, develop a sensor for it, and run a
screening programme in three clinics."

**Good:** "We will develop a sensor for protein P so that clinic screening can
detect disease D a median of 14 months earlier than the current blood panel."

## Aims, objectives, and methods are three different things

These words get used interchangeably and the resulting mush is a leading
rejection cause. They are a hierarchy, and the panel checks that the hierarchy
is intact.

| Layer | Answers | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Why does anyone care? The change in the world | Falsifiable in principle, reachable in the grant period |
| Objective | What will be produced? The concrete deliverable | A thing that can be marked done |
| Method | How? The procedure, sample, and analysis | Reproducible by a competent stranger |

Common failures at each layer:

- An aim written as an activity ("This project aims to study X") states no
  outcome. Rewrite as what will be known afterwards that is not known now.
- An objective with no termination condition ("explore whether X relates to
  Y") can absorb any amount of time and is unfalsifiable as a promise. Give
  it a deliverable with a number or a named artefact attached.
- A method with no measurement plan is close to the worst individual defect a
  proposal can carry. "We will assess the effect of X on Y" says nothing
  until the reviewer knows what instrument measures Y, what effect size is
  detectable, and what happens if the result is null. No measurement plan is
  a rejection, not a weakness the panel forgives.

Structure aims so each one could fail and the others still stand, where the
science allows it. Interdependent aims mean one early result falling drops the
whole programme; name the dependency and the fallback aim where dependency is
unavoidable.

## The significance paragraph for a panel outside your subfield

The panel knows your field at the level of a good annual review, not your
subfield's open problems. Two sentences of setup in your vocabulary are noise
to them; the significance you leave unsaid is invisible.

- State the problem in terms a non-specialist scientist in your broad field
  recognises: a patient outcome, a cost, an unresolved mechanism, a class of
  systems that fails. Then say what stands in the way.
- Never write "little is known about X" as the gap claim. Panels read it as
  "the applicant has not done the reading". The gap is a limitation of current
  work, with the citation that shows current work hit it: "Current methods
  fail above temperature T (Smith et al., ref 14); whether this is a material
  limit or a fabrication artefact is unresolved, and both answers imply
  different engineering."
- Name who uses the result and what they do differently if you succeed. A
  significance statement without a named user of the knowledge reads as
  curiosity without a case.
- One mechanism per sentence. Panels skim, and a sentence carrying three
  nested clauses gets read at clause one.

## Feasibility and track record without overclaiming

The proposal must argue you can do it. The two failure modes are opposite:
claiming the outcome is certain (not research), or leaving the panel to
infer capability (not evidence). The working tone is "here is our evidence
that the risky parts are bounded".

- Preliminary data exist to retire risk, not to show off. Point each
  preliminary figure at the specific aim risk it retires: "Pilot data (Fig. 2)
  show the assay detects the signal at the concentration our Aim 1 requires."
- De-risk the schedule the panel already distrusts: recruitment, custom
  instruments, cross-site data sharing, ethics approval. For each, name the
  plan, the alternative, and evidence it has been done before.
- Frame trained-personnel risk with names and fractional effort, not "an
  experienced postdoctoral researcher will be hired". A named person with two
  relevant publications beats an anonymous capability claim.
- Where yours is genuinely the first attempt at something, say why now: what
  changed (a technology, a dataset, a regulatory shift) that makes it feasible
  this year and was not before.
- Never promise a result. "If Aim 1 succeeds, Aim 2 tests whether X" is
  credible; "Aims 1 to 3 will establish that X causes Y" is a proposal with
  the research already written into the past tense. Panels mark this as
  promise inflation and discount everything else.

## The budget is an argument, line by line

The budget justification is not an accounting document; it is the
feasibility case in dollar form. Every line answers "why this amount, why
this item, and what happens to the science if it is cut".

- Tie each personnel line to an aim by fraction: a named role, a percentage
  of effort, and the aims that percentage serves. An unexplained full-time
  person on a two-aim grant gets a panel question about padding.
- Justify major equipment by the measurement it enables and by why the
  existing institutional one cannot do it (throughput, access, contamination
  risk, a specification difference named precisely).
- Travel: name the reason, not the destination. "Two annual meetings of the
  consortium that governs data sharing agreement A" justifies; "conference
  attendance" does not.
- Animals or human subjects, where present: per-unit costs must match the
  sample size in the methods. A budget of 200 mice under methods reading
  n=40 is the inconsistency panels remember.
- Keep the indirect-rate arithmetic honest and let the institution's grants
  office check it. Errors here go past reviewers to research administrators,
  who remember.

## Letters of support do actual work

A letter is evidence, not ceremony. What it must do:

- Commit, not praise. "We will provide 20% of beam time at no cost" is a
  letter that changes the feasibility score. "Dr. X's group is world-leading"
  changes nothing, because the panel has already judged that from the CV.
- Get them from the people who own the resource the proposal depends on, not
  from famous people who own nothing the proposal needs.
- Write the requester a one-page memo of what the proposal needs from them,
  so the letter commits to the right thing. Allowing a busy supporter to
  improvise usually produces the praise-only letter.
- Check the funder's rules: some cap the count, some require a specific form,
  some forbid letters from co-investigators' own institutions.

## Write for the reading conditions

The panel reads in the worst conditions documents are ever read: many
proposals per reviewer, minutes per proposal at the meeting, a scoring sheet
open beside the PDF. The document must work when skimmed.

- The abstract is the highest-leverage paragraph in the document. Reviewers
  assigned to your panel read it; reviewers not assigned skim it before the
  discussion vote; the panel's written summary is often drafted from it. If
  one paragraph gets all your revision effort, it is this one, written last.
- After the abstract, reviewers read the specific aims page, then jump to
  what their expertise covers. Each section must therefore survive being
  entered cold: open it with the sentence that orients a reader who skipped
  the two before it.
- Front-load per section: the finding or the commitment in the first
  sentence, the qualification second. Do not build to a conclusion; a skim
  reads only the openings, so openings carry the argument.
- Headings should be claims where the format allows ("Aim 2 tests whether
  the effect persists at clinical dose"), not labels ("Aim 2"). A reader
  scanning only headings then walks away with your argument.
- If the funder permits it, a figure showing the design (aims, sample, and
  measurements as a diagram) earns more panel comprehension per unit space
  than any other element in the document.

## Timeline realism

Panels do not reward optimism; they reward evidence the schedule was built
from durations rather than wishes.

- Schedule reverse-order precision: put your worst, roundest estimates on the
  earliest phases, because recruitment and setup overrun, not writing.
- Put milestones on the timeline that produce an inspectable artefact: a
  registered protocol, a validated instrument, a first data batch. A
  milestone that is "complete analysis" cannot be tracked; one that is
  "dataset of n=200 locked and code released" can.
- Add the downside path for each phase-2-and-later risk: what happens to
  months 24 to 36 if recruitment hits 60% of target. A proposal with a
  contingency paragraph reads as run by someone who has run a project before.
- Do not show a timeline where every phase is sequential and none overlaps.
  Real projects overlap; a fully serial plan reads as the first schedule the
  author drew.

## Broader impact and ethics sections

Funders increasingly score these explicitly, which makes them scoreable
content, not boilerplate.

- Broader impact claims need named mechanisms: who is trained, what is
  shared where, which community is engaged and by what channel. "Results
  will be disseminated widely" scores nothing anywhere.
- Promise only what someone in the proposal is accountable for. A broader
  impact of "training two PhD students from the listed recruitment
  programme" is checkable; "inspiring future scientists" is not.
- Data and code sharing: name the repository, the licence, and the timing
  relative to publication. A sharing statement with no named home reads as
  written to satisfy the form.
- Write the ethics section for the actual risk of your actual study
  population, including the risks of collecting nothing where that applies
  (data governance when reusing records). The panel that spots a copied
  ethics section marks the whole application down.

## Why proposals get rejected

The recurring reasons, in rough order of how often they decide:

| Reason | What the panel writes | The check before submitting |
|---|---|---|
| Vague aims | "Aims are not clearly defined" | Each aim has a deliverable and a number |
| No measurement plan | "Approach lacks detail on how outcomes are assessed" | Instrument, size, and null-result handling stated per aim |
| Not significant to the panel | "The importance of the problem is not established" | A non-specialist panel member says why they care in one sentence |
| Unjustified budget | "Budget does not align with the proposed work" | Every line tied to an aim and a fraction |
| Overambition | "Too much for the period" | The one-sentence description survived; timeline has fallbacks |
| Weak fit | "Better suited to another programme" | Three funded abstracts from this programme were read first |
| Track record unclear | "Does not establish capacity to deliver" | Named people, named tools, preliminary data pointing at risks |

## Resubmission discipline

Most programmes fund more revised proposals than first submissions. The
resubmission is a different document with a different opening move.

- Read the reviews as data about the reader, not about your worth. Sort each
  comment into three piles: fixable in text, fixable with new work, and restates
  a constraint you cannot change. All three get responses; only the first two
  get changes.
- Respond point by point in the funder's required format, quoting the
  comment, stating the change, and giving its location. A reply answering a
  subset reads as evasion, exactly as in paper rebuttals.
- The first page carries the headline of what changed. Reviewers reread the
  abstract and aims first; a summary of changes up front saves everyone the
  hunt and sets the frame before they reach any old wound.
- For a criticism you disagree with, address the substance and restate the
  concern in your own words. If two reviewers contradicted each other, name
  the contradiction and state which resolution you adopted and why, which is
  the only way to satisfy both.
- Do not resubmit with the summary criticism visibly unaddressed, even if you
  think the reviewer was wrong. The same reviewer often rereads it. Change the
  document so the concern is answered by the text itself, not only by the
  response letter.

## Before you submit

1. Does the project survive compression to one sentence with no "and"?
2. Does each aim name its deliverable, its instrument, and its size?
3. Does the null result of each aim have a stated interpretation?
4. Could a panel member outside the subfield restate the significance from
   the first page?
5. Does the budget justify every line by aim and fraction?
6. Does every preliminary figure point at the risk it retires?
7. Does the abstract carry the strongest version of the whole case?
8. Does the tracking table for the reviews exist and have three columns for
   the resubmission that may come?
