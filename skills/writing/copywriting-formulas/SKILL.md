# Copywriting formulas

Persuasive copy has a job: the reader takes an action. Formulas are the
tested scaffolds that make that likely. This skill picks the right one
for the goal and enforces the structure that makes it work.

The formulas are not magic. They work because they respect a real order
of operations: attention, then relevance, then belief, then action. A
formula that skips a step fails at the first paragraph.

## Pick the formula from the goal, not the format

| Goal | Formula | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Land a new reader / cold audience | **AIDA** | Attention gates everything; assumes zero prior context. |
| Sell a solution to a known pain | **PAS** | The pain does the persuading; you only have to name it accurately. |
| Change a mind that already read a blog post | **BAB** (Before / After / Bridge) | Contrasts the reader's current state with the promised one. |
| A product page for a specific feature | **4 Ps** (Promise / Picture / Proof / Push) | Structured around belief; proof is where the sale is made. |
| A landing page for a specific campaign | **PASTOR** (Problem / Amplify / Story / Transformation / Offer / Response) | Long-form path when the ask is high. |
| An urgent post: a limited launch, a critical fix | **QUEST** (Qualify / Understand / Educate / Stimulate / Transition) | Qualify first to keep the wrong reader out. |

If two formulas would fit, pick the shorter one. Length dilutes.

## AIDA: the default for cold audiences

- **Attention.** One line, concrete. Not clever, not clickbait; specific.
  Bad: "Unlock your team's potential." Good: "Your on-call team owes
  itself 7.3 hours a week."
- **Interest.** The reason this reader specifically should keep reading.
  Named audience, named context.
- **Desire.** The picture of the outcome, in the reader's terms. Not
  features. A specific week that looks different.
- **Action.** Exactly one ask. Measured verb. Not "learn more"; "book
  a 20 minute call with an on-call engineer this week".

## PAS: for a known pain

- **Problem.** State the pain the reader already feels. If the reader
  disagrees with this line, the whole piece is dead.
- **Agitate.** Make the cost of not solving it concrete: hours, dollars,
  weekends. Not adjectives.
- **Solve.** The intervention. Named, specific, not "our platform".

PAS lives or dies on the Problem line. If you cannot state it in a
sentence the reader would nod at, do more research before writing.

## The promise test

Every formula has an implicit promise. Read the first paragraph and ask:
what did I just promise the reader? Read the last paragraph and ask:
did I keep it?

If the answers do not match, you have written a bait-and-switch. Rewrite,
do not paper over.

## Specificity beats sophistication

Numbers over adjectives. A named place over "somewhere". A quoted line
over a paraphrase. The best copy sounds like the target reader thinking
out loud.

Bad: "Our platform saves teams significant time."

Good: "The last team we worked with cut Tuesday's stand-up from 45
minutes to 12."

## Objections belong in the middle, not the FAQ

If the reader has an objection you know about, name it before they close
the tab. "You are wondering whether this works for a team of three. It
does; here is the smallest deployment we shipped last quarter."

An FAQ at the bottom means you have moved the objections out of the sale.
The reader who has the objection already left.

## One call to action

Two CTAs are a coin flip; three are a shrug. Pick the single action the
reader should take and put every path in the page through it. Secondary
paths belong on the next page, not this one.

## Read it out loud

A sentence that trips your tongue trips the reader's eye. Contractions
are usually right. If it sounds like a business memo, it reads like a
business memo, which is not what converts.

## Common pitfalls

Bad: opening with `In today's fast-paced world` or an industry stat.

Good: opening with a specific line the reader would recognise from their
own week.

Bad: writing features. "Real-time dashboards. Custom alerts. SSO."

Good: writing outcomes. "Your ops lead stops checking the pager on
Sundays."

Bad: hedging the CTA. "Feel free to reach out."

Good: a measured verb. "Reply with the size of your on-call rotation."

Bad: writing to a persona invented in a workshop.

Good: writing to one real person you interviewed last month.

Bad: adjective stacking. `A revolutionary, game-changing solution.`

Good: a number. "Ten thousand teams use this to run stand-up."

Bad: a wall of testimonials at the bottom.

Good: one line of proof next to each claim.

## When not to use this

- Long-form journalism. Different craft; conversion is not the goal.
- Technical reference docs. The reader is not deciding; they are looking
  something up.
- Anything internal where the reader has to read it anyway. Formulas
  optimise for the reader who could close the tab.
