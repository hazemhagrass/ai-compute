# Newsletter Writing

A newsletter is a promise someone accepted on purpose. Every issue either keeps
that promise or spends trust down. Optimize for the reader finishing the issue,
not for the number of links or sections it contains.

## One message, one purpose

Decide in one sentence, before drafting, what the issue asks the reader to
know or do. An issue with two purposes delivers neither: the reader stops at
whichever one they hit first.

Bad:

> A deep dive on index design, a pricing change, three talks, a coupon, and a
> survey.

Good:

> This month: one deep dive on index design. Everything else is a footnote.

A genuinely urgent second item earns one labeled line at the top, not a
co-equal section.

## The subject line is the whole pitch

The reader decides from the subject and preview text; the body is what happens
after. Write a specific promise, short enough to survive truncation, honest
about what is inside. Bait earns one open and a permanent mute.

Bad:

> You won't believe what changed in Postgres this month
> Fwd: Incredible Newsletter #17

Good:

> Why Postgres rejects your index: B-tree size limits, in 5 minutes
> The one query that changed our latency budget

Rules:

- Specific beats clever: name the fact, the number, or the mistake.
- Under roughly 60 characters; if front-of-inbox truncation cuts it, the cut
  should not remove the promise.
- No clickbait, no "you won't believe", no capitalized word spam, no fake
  reply chains or "Fwd:".
- When stuck, a literal summary of the main section is a safe subject line:
  it is honest whether it gets opened or skipped.

## Preview text finishes what the subject line starts

Preview text is free pitch space. Leaving it to "View in browser" or the
opening of the legal footer throws away half the decision.

Bad:

> View this email in your browser... This newsletter was sent to you because...

Good:

> Subject: Why Postgres rejects your index
> Preview: the B-tree limit is 2704 bytes, and here is what to do instead

The preview continues the subject line's promise instead of repeating it. Set
it explicitly in the platform rather than letting the client scrape whatever
sits at the top of the body.

## Body structure: the idea up top, links curated

The first screen carries the single idea, stated once, using the reader's own
problem as the example. Then a small set of links, each with your one-line
reason for keeping it. Curating is filtering; a heap of links teaches readers
to scan past the whole section.

Bad:

> - A bunch of links
> - 20 links pasted in a heap, two of which you know are actually good
> - zero reasons to click any of them

Good:

> - Three links, each with one line saying what it is and why you kept it
> - Your own verdict even when it is "this article is wrong about X, but the
>   numbers section is worth it"

Rules:

- If you cannot write one useful sentence about a link, cut it.
- Archive old issues so a prospective subscriber can read the voice first.
- Close with the same signal every issue so readers learn the rhythm.

## The ratio of original writing to curation

Curation alone competes with RSS and adds nothing. Whatever split you pick,
do not let the original share hit zero across consecutive issues: the reader
subscribed for a judgement they cannot get from an aggregator.

Bad:

> Issue after issue of pure link dumps with no stance from the author

Good:

> A written lead, then a curated second act where each kept item carries your
> verdict rather than its own headline.

Rule:

- Judge each issue not by "did I write prose" but by "did I put in a sentence
  that could not have been generated by copying a source".

## Voice consistency across issues

The reader subscribed to a person's perspective, not a topic. Reuse the same
greeting, the same running references, the same pronouns. Drift reads as a
handover to someone else, not as growth.

Bad:

> Issue 1: first person, contractions, inside jokes. Issue 4: "we are pleased
> to announce."

Good:

> The same greeting, contractions, and stance every issue: the mood varies,
> the voice does not.

Rules:

- Write the first issue in your normal voice, not "newsletter voice".
- Keep a runbook of the repeats (greeting, sign-off, running bits) and consult
  it each issue rather than your memory.
- Keep the reply address and tracking stance consistent too: readers read
  those as part of the voice.

## Segment your list for content, not for spray

Blasting everything to everyone teaches readers the sends are not addressed
to them. Segment by what a reader can act on, not by who you wish they were.

Bad:

> One send to everyone: the beginner leaves over the deep article, the expert
> leaves over the introductory one.

Good:

> Two sends from the same template, one each per interest segment; replies
> go up in both, because the whole issue is aimed at one thing each reader
> already signed up for.

Rules:

- Split by intent the reader declared at signup, never by inferred traits.
- Send only to the segment the issue actually addresses; make the send-to-all
  call per issue, not by default.

## A send cadence you can sustain beats a cadence you can respect

Rhythm matters more than frequency: weekly-then-stopped is worse than
fortnightly-every-time. Work back from how many issues you can write at full
quality, then pick a cadence slower than the point where writing gets rushed.

Bad:

> "We will send this every Tuesday, Monday, and Friday. Daily. 3am."

Good:

> One issue per week, written in one fixed block, sent at that same fixed
> block, with a two-issue backlog banked against the week you cannot write.

Rules:

- Publish no cadence until three issues are banked: a named rhythm on the
  signup page is a promise.
- A missed issue is a trust event: name it in one line next issue rather than
  going quiet.

## Plain text vs HTML: choose knowingly

HTML costs deliverability and renders differently in every client; plain text
avoids that and reads like a personal note, losing only design control. Choose
deliberately rather than taking the platform default.

Bad:

> A columned HTML template with background images, sent to nine readers.

Good:

> Plain text with one link per line; or a single-column template tested in
> Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail with images blocked.

Rules:

- Run the images-off test every time. An issue that is invisible without
  hosted images is an issue that lost.
- WCAG applies to email: screen readers reach newsletters through the same
  clients as everyone else, so semantic headings and alt text matter.
- Check selector support in real clients before styling; email CSS is a tiny,
  weird subset of the web platform.

## The unsubscribe placement is a trust signal, not a legal footnote

The unsubscribe is part of the design: visible, one click, no "log in to
manage preferences first". An unsubscribe that requires detective work turns
a subscriber who merely wanted out into one who reports you as junk.

Bad:

> A 6px footer line the same color as the background, linking to a preference
> center that wants a password.

Good:

> "One click to unsubscribe: [link]", with the preference center offered as
> an optional second step.

Rules:

- Some readers want less, not none: offer a pause or a reduced cadence.
- Set the List-Unsubscribe headers (RFC 2369, RFC 8058) so the client shows
  its own native unsubscribe control, which readers trust most.

## List hygiene and a sunset policy

A list that only grows is a liability: unengaged addresses raise the
complaint rate per send, which is the signal providers weigh most. Set the
sunset rule before you have an incentive not to apply it.

Bad:

> Keeping the ancient signups who have not opened in years, because the list
> number on the pitch page would drop.

Good:

> No opens or clicks in the last 90 days: promote them once ("still want
> this?"), then remove the non-responders without another campaign.

Rules:

- Run the sweep on a fixed schedule and write the rule down next to the
  cadence you committed to.
- Clean bounces and role addresses promptly, since an uncleaned list repeats
  the same failure on every send at rising cost.

## Metrics: opens are a weak proxy

Opens were never a good proxy for reading: clients vary in whether they load
tracking pixels at all, and some preload every image regardless of whether a
human looked. Optimize towards signals that require deliberate action from a
reader, which means replies, link clicks, and the unsubscribe-versus-engaged
ratio. Compare issues against your own trend rather than an absolute number.

Bad:

> "Open rate went up this month, so the subject line formula works."

Good:

> "Three replies and 22 link clicks on a list of 180: this issue hit."
> Plus, in the same sentence, one qualitative reply quoted, even unnamed.

Rules:

- Track your own trend before comparing to industry benchmarks drawn from
  lists nothing like yours.
- Clicks and replies are the signals that matter, and unsubscribes matter as
  much as either: a list that never loses anyone is rarely being read.

## Answering replies is the best research channel

Replies are a reader raising their hand. They are the highest-signal source
of future topics, and the reason to publish from a working address at all.

Bad:

> A no-reply address, or an inbox nobody reads behind a "not monitored" note.

Good:

> A working reply address answered by a human within a day or two, and an
> occasional note saying what a reply changed about this issue.

Rules:

- Keep the one-line "what did you think?" close, and read what comes back.
- Log the ideas replies generate: the best issue of the quarter often starts
  as one.
- Never answer a reply with an auto-responder or a feedback form: the gesture
  was a conversation, and a scripted reply ends it.
