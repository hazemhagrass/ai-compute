# Social Media Scheduler

A scheduling plan is a promise to an audience, not a content dump. The platform
shapes the post, the calendar shapes the week, and the ratio shapes whether
people stay.

## Platform-native beats cross-posting

Each platform has its own reading contract: length, register, what counts as a
hook, what counts as an ending. A copy-pasted cross-post reads wrong on every
platform it lands on, and audiences can tell, because they are fluent in their
home platform and you are visiting.

Bad: write one 280-character paragraph and paste it to four networks, then
wonder why engagement differs by an order of magnitude between them.

Good: draft per platform, even when the underlying message is identical. Same
announcement, three shapes:

- The thread platform: one claim per post, numbered, last post carries the
  takeaway and the link.
- The short-video platform: hook in the first two seconds, text on screen,
  no link the viewer has to leave to follow.
- The link-preview platform: a clean title, a real description, and an image
  sized for the card, because the card is the post.

If you truly have no budget for native drafts, post to the two platforms that
matter and skip the rest. A good post on two platforms beats a recycled one on
five.

## The calendar is a rolling week, not a year

Queue one week ahead. Farther than that, posts get scheduled before you know
what you will want to say, and scheduled content has a way of going out
mid-crisis looking like it was written by someone who was not paying attention.

- Keep a backlog of ideas (any length) and a queue of scheduled posts (one
  week, concrete, drafted).
- Each week: pull the best backlog items into next week's queue, review the
  week just posted for what actually worked, and leave slack. A queue filled
  to 100% cannot absorb a timely post.
- Time-sensitive content (news reactions, event announcements) is drafted and
  posted live, or it is not timely anymore.

Bad: an auto-queue twelve weeks deep of "5 tips" posts, with a customer
disaster unfolding while a cheerful tip about the product lands.

Good: seven scheduled slots, three backlog candidates, one slot deliberately
empty for whatever the week produces.

## Content ratio: mostly theirs, some yours

Followers tolerate a promotion feed the way readers tolerate an ad-broken page:
quietly, and then not at all. Aim for the majority of posts sharing something
useful with no ask (a lesson, a link to someone else's work, a behind-the-scenes
detail), and a minority promoting your own thing. The exact ratio is yours to
set; the direction is not negotiable. If you cannot say what a follower got
from your last five posts, the ratio has slipped.

A practical split that keeps itself honest: for every self-promotional post,
draft one useful post you could have promoted with instead, and post it first.

## Hooks differ by platform type

Text platforms pay out on the first line: the hook is a sentence, and the
promise must be kept by the post that follows. Visual platforms pay out on the
first frame: the hook is an image, a face, a bold claim on screen, and sound
carries the promise.

- Text hook template: state the surprising claim or the concrete number, then
  earn it. "We cut auth latency 60% by deleting code" is a hook; "Some thoughts
  on performance" is not.
- Visual hook template: the viewer should be able to answer "what is this and
  why do I care" before the sound starts. On-screen text stating the payoff,
  in the first frame, before any title card.
- Never write a hook the body contradicts. The next post in the thread and the
  tenth second of the video both punish the bait.

## Repurpose honestly: fragments, not photocopies

One long piece (a talk, a doc, a release) genuinely becomes a week of posts.
But each fragment must stand alone and be shaped for where it lands.

- Pull out the claims that survive without the surrounding context: a number
  with its comparison, a before/after, one lesson with one example.
- Cut the connective tissue ("as I said earlier", "in part one"). A fragment
  that starts "thirdly" was not made for the platform it is on.
- Attribute inside the fragment: "from our new guide" beats a naked claim,
  and a naked screenshot of a paragraph from your own blog is the laziest
  form this can take; the platform-native rewrite is the job.
- If a point only worked inside the long piece, drop it rather than maroon it.

## The reply window is part of the post

Posting is the start of the commitment, not the end. An account that posts and
vanishes teaches the audience that replies are invisible, and the following
count quietly reflects that.

- Keep the hour after posting for replies on the platform's native surface.
  Re-share, answer, and engage with the comments as they arrive, in the same
  voice as the post itself.
- Replies are content too. A good reply is seen by everyone who sees the post,
  so it deserves the same care: specific, generous, no "Great question!"
- If you cannot staff the window, schedule for a time you can, rather than
  posting into silence.

## Timing claims: measure, do not recite

You will encounter confident folklore about the best time to post, usually
with precise numbers attached. Treat all of it as folklore. Platform feeds,
audience time zones, and algorithm weightings differ per account, and the
published numbers came from someone else's audience, aggregated until it was
nobody's.

Bad: "Post at 11am on Wednesday because engagement is 40% higher."

Good: "Assume no magic time. Post at a few different times that are plausible
for your audience's time zones, log each post's reach and engagement with its
time slot, and compare after a few weeks of your own data. Re-test when the
platform changes, because it will."

The claim you are allowed to make is about method, not numbers: test and
measure on your own audience. Logging the slot alongside the outcome is the
cheapest possible version of this and it is enough.

## Automation ethics

Automation is legitimate for scheduling, cross-portfolio order, and reminders.
It stops being legitimate the moment it manufactures social proof or fakes a
person.

- Never buy or bot engagement: fake likes, fake followers, engagement pods
  that exist only to inflate metrics, or reply bots that pretend to be
  customers. Platforms detect it, audience members detect it, and both
  detections cost more than the metrics were worth.
- Never automate a persona. If a bot accounts exist, label it as a bot.
  Automated accounts that hold conversations as if they were people are
  deception regardless of how good the product is.
- Auto-replies to comments are a fake conversation. An acknowledgement pitch
  that says "thanks for engaging!" under every comment is worse than silence.
- Scheduled tools are fine. The author reads, decides, and writes; the tool
  only moves the post in time.

## Quick checklist

- Drafted per platform, not pasted across platforms.
- Queue is one week deep with one open slot.
- This week's ratio skim: mostly value, some promotion, asks clearly marked.
- Every hook is kept by its body.
- Fragments stand alone, attribute their source.
- Reply window staffed or the slot moved.
- No invented timing percentages anywhere near the plan.
- No fake engagement, no unlabeled bots.
